The thing I'd push back on first, gently, is the framing itself. You've described this as "pub/sub vs streaming-first," but the shape of your problem is actually "one pipeline, twenty consumers, and those twenty consumers are not the same workload." Audit log wants durability and replayability. Alerting wants low latency and deduplication. ML features want event-time joins and clean backfill. BI wants batch-shaped aggregates. Analytics wants ad-hoc flexibility. No single substrate is equally good at all of those — and when a team argues about substrate, it's often because the consumer portfolio hasn't been laid out side-by-side yet. So before the architecture debate, I'd do the exercise of listing all 20 (or the 20 planned) against four columns: latency requirement, consistency requirement, replay/backfill need, and who owns operating it. My strong suspicion is that the distribution is bimodal — most consumers just want "give me the events reliably," and a small number (maybe 3-5) genuinely need stateful stream processing with event-time semantics.

If that turns out to be true, then the two camps are both right about different halves of the problem, and the architecture follows:

**Log as substrate, streaming as an opt-in layer on top.** Pick a durable, replayable log first — Kafka (or Redpanda if you want Kafka API without the ZK/KRaft operational weight, or NATS JetStream if you want something lighter and your retention/throughput needs are modest). That log is the source of truth. The 15-ish consumers that just need "events, reliably, with replay" consume it directly, which is exactly what your senior backend engineers are comfortable operating. Then for the 3-5 consumers that genuinely need materialized state with exactly-once and event-time guarantees — probably ML features and alerting-with-dedup — put a streaming engine (Flink, or Arroyo if you want a lighter operational footprint, or RisingWave / Materialize if a SQL-native materialized-view model fits) *on top of the log*, writing derived outputs back to the log or to a serving store. You are not choosing between the two architectures; you are layering them, with the log as the invariant.

Why this handles the real tensions rather than pretending they aren't there: your backend team's concern isn't wrong — 5M events/day is ~60/sec average, and bringing up a streaming framework to serve workloads that a simple consumer would serve is genuine over-engineering, and streaming frameworks have sharp operational edges (checkpoint tuning, state backend sizing, watermark debugging) that punish teams without prior scars. Your data platform lead's concern also isn't wrong — if ML features and alerting are built on ad-hoc consumer code doing windowing by hand, you'll accrue consistency debt that compounds silently until someone traces a mispredicted alert back to a race in a consumer nobody remembers writing. The layered approach lets the team grow into streaming against a bounded, small surface area where the guarantees genuinely earn their cost, while the log-as-substrate keeps the other 80% of consumers in territory the team already knows.

A few things to stress-test this against. First, the volume number deserves a second look — 5M events/day average hides peak shape. If your peak is 50x average (say, a nightly batch from an upstream system), sizing changes. Second, watch for the trap of letting "we'll add streaming later" become "we never did and now three consumers each built their own broken windowing." Pick the first streaming use-case up front and commit to it, so the streaming layer earns a real operator and real on-call rotation rather than being an unowned experiment. Third, resist the urge to let the streaming engine become the log. Materialized-view engines are seductive because they feel like "the whole pipeline in one place," but they tend to be bad at the things logs are good at — long retention, cheap replay, being the source of truth that survives your framework choice. Keep them as derivation layers, not substrates.

One cheap move to unblock the team conversation: rather than debating the architecture abstractly, pick the single hardest consumer (probably ML features or alerting) and the single easiest (probably audit log or raw analytics sink) and ask each camp to sketch how they'd build both. The split usually dissolves once both camps see that their preferred tool is strictly better for one end of the spectrum and strictly worse for the other. The disagreement is real, but it's a disagreement about *weighting the portfolio*, not about which architecture wins in the abstract — and weighting a portfolio is a much more tractable conversation than picking a winner.
